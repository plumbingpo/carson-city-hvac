From the hallway, a furnace that will not heat looks the same no matter what broke. Inside the cabinet, though, everything happens in a strict order: inducer on, pressure switch closed, igniter glowing, gas valve open, burners lit, flame proven, and finally the blower moving heat through the ducts. Most of the diagnosis comes down to finding the broken link. In Carson City homes, it tends to be the same handful of culprits: a flame sensor coated in oxide, a hot surface igniter cracked by thermal cycling, a pressure switch held open by a tired inducer or a plugged condensate trap, or a control board with a burned relay.

Rather than guess, Carson City HVAC tests the sequence one stage at a time. We read igniter resistance, measure flame signal in microamps, check that the pressure switch responds to real inducer draft, confirm voltage at the gas valve, and test the blower and its capacitor. Because this is a fuel-burning appliance, the safety basics come with every visit: the heat exchanger gets inspected, gas connections get leak-tested, and draft gets verified. You cannot tell a furnace problem from a furnace hazard by looking at the thermostat. Before we leave, temperature rise is measured against the data plate so the fire and the airflow are balanced as the manufacturer intended.

We work on gas, propane, and electric models from every major brand, both conventional 80 percent units and condensing high-efficiency models with their traps, drains, and dual pressure switches. The truck stock covers the parts that actually fail: igniters, flame sensors, pressure switches, capacitors, inducer assemblies, and blower motors, so most repairs finish in one trip. Warning Signs for Furnace Repair

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you time, money, and prevent significant damage to your property. Keep an eye out for the following clues that it's time to bring in an expert:

  • A fan that keeps running while the registers blow cool air
  • The igniter glows and clicks, yet no flame ever appears
  • Yellow, wavering burner flames where they should be sharp blue
  • Short cycling every few minutes without reaching the setpoint
  • Booming or banging at light-off from delayed ignition
  • Squealing or grinding from the blower compartment
  • Flames that start, last a few seconds, and go out again and again
  • Water, rust stains, or black soot around the flue or cabinet

Why Professional Furnace Repair Matters

Gas, open flame, high voltage, and a flue all share one cabinet in a furnace, and the most dangerous failure is the one you cannot see: a damaged heat exchanger that lets combustion gases into the air the blower circulates. Every morning that delayed ignition keeps booming at startup, that exchanger takes more stress. The diagnostic side has its own pitfalls. When a furnace trips its high limit, the switch itself may be bad, or the furnace may be protecting itself from a clogged filter or a failing blower. Replacing the switch when the real issue is airflow removes a safety device and leaves the actual problem in place.

Our experienced technicians look at every symptom in the context of the whole furnace. We measure flame signal instead of assuming it, which keeps us from replacing a healthy sensor while a cracked igniter or weak gas valve goes unnoticed. Draft is confirmed at the pressure switch and at the flue. Temperature rise is checked against the data plate, catching airflow problems before they cut years off the heat exchanger. The gas side is leak-tested with instruments, never with a quick sniff. The result is a furnace proven safe over a full cycle, plus an honest opinion if the unit is getting close to the point where replacing it beats repairing it again.

How to Prevent Furnace Repair Problems

A few simple habits now, like regular filter changes and seasonal tune-ups, can add years to your system's life. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Change the filter monthly during heating season; it is the cheapest furnace part you will ever buy
  • Schedule service each fall to catch worn igniters and dirty sensors before cold weather arrives
  • Avoid packing the utility room, because burners need open space for combustion air
  • Store laundry supplies, especially chlorine products, well away from the furnace closet
  • If you have a high-efficiency furnace, check that the condensate drain is flowing freely
  • Test your CO detectors monthly and make sure each floor has one
  • Notice how the furnace sounds at startup, because a quiet light-off is healthy and a boom is not
